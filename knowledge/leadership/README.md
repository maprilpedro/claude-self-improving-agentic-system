# Leadership — Operating at Senior Director Level

> Patterns, behaviors, and insights on how Senior Directors operate differently from Directors. Not about doing more — about operating at a different scope.

## The Core Distinction

Directors own their product area. Senior Directors shape decisions across areas they don't directly own. The difference is not capability. It is the scope of what you claim as yours to solve.

## Cross-Org Influence Without Authority

### Pick Up the Open Action Item
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Loni's AEM PM Virtual Working Session IV (Surfaces). Loni named the "hero surfaces / Times Square" concept and left the definition open as an action item. Pedro took it.
- **Pattern**: In leadership sessions, the most valuable moves are often the ones nobody claimed. When a VP names a concept and doesn't assign it, that is an invitation. The person who walks out and writes the first draft owns the framing. Framing is authority.
- **How to recognize it**: Look for concepts named in a session with no clear owner. Open action items with strategic weight. Ideas the facilitator floated but didn't assign.
- **How to execute**: Write the one-pager fast. Validate with your manager before sharing broadly. Bring it back to the next session with your name and your manager's name on it.
- **Why it works**: You're not claiming territory you don't own. You're filling a vacuum that exists because senior leaders don't have time to do it themselves. They want someone to. Be that person.

### Validate with N Before Sharing to N+1
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-24
- **Source**: Agent dashboard standardization plan. Pedro validated with Bertrand first ("Fully Aligned") before sending to the broader Slack channel.
- **Pattern**: Any proposal going to a broader audience should be pre-validated with your direct manager first. This does two things: (1) you don't get publicly corrected by your own chain, which is damaging; (2) your manager becomes a co-owner, which amplifies the signal — it arrives backed by two levels of the org, not one.
- **Anti-pattern**: Sending something directly to a VP or senior audience without looping in your manager. Even if the content is good, the process signal is bad.

### Get Leadership's Name Alongside Yours
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Hero Surfaces one-pager. Third ask to Bertrand: "Permission to bring this to Loni's next working session as a position, with your name on it alongside mine."
- **Pattern**: When you're taking something to a skip-level, your manager's name on the document is not just courtesy — it's structural legitimacy. It signals alignment, not just advocacy. And it gives your manager a stake in the outcome, which makes them more likely to support it actively rather than passively.
- **Nuance**: Only ask for this when you genuinely have alignment. If Bertrand hasn't validated the content, don't claim his name. That backfires fast.

## Strategic Narrative

### Lead with "So What," Not "What Happened"
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Director PM framework, reinforced across Loni's sessions.
- **Pattern**: Directors report what happened. Senior Directors explain why it matters and what comes next. The same facts can be presented at two very different levels:
  - Director: "EPA had a 9% success rate in W12, down from 43% all-time."
  - Senior Director: "EPA's 9% external success rate is not a quality problem — it's an architecture ceiling. AO 2.0 addresses the root cause. Until it lands (May-July), no prompt improvements will move this number materially. The right investment is measurement standardization, not agent tuning."
- **Application**: Before any written or verbal update, ask: "What does this mean and what should we do about it?" Lead with that. Put the data second.

### Connect Every Artifact to the Company Direction
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Loni's use of Project 42 as the organizing frame across multiple sessions.
- **Pattern**: The most durable strategic documents reference the company-level frame explicitly. In AEM, that frame is Project 42 and the content management → context management direction. When your work connects visibly to that frame, it reads as strategy. When it doesn't, it reads as tactics.
- **How to use**: End the opening paragraph of any proposal with one sentence connecting it to the larger frame. "This positions Experience Hub as the hero surface for the human practitioner layer of Project 42's content-to-context transition."

## Stakeholder Gravity

### Be in the Room Where Concepts Are Named
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Pedro's inclusion in Loni's Session I and Session IV. Both small, senior, first-principles sessions.
- **Pattern**: Senior Directors are sought out for strategy sessions. Getting into those rooms is not automatic — it requires that you've already demonstrated you can operate at that level. Being in the room is a lagging indicator; contributing visibly while in the room is a leading indicator of being invited back.
- **What "contributing visibly" looks like**: Proposing a framing that gets repeated by others. Asking the question that reorients the discussion. Not talking the most — saying the one thing that makes the room think differently.
- **Example**: Pedro's persona-based interaction model (Session I, March 23) was validated by Conrad in front of Loni. That's a contribution that gets remembered and attributed.

### Your Point Gets Echoed = Credibility Signal
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Session IV (Surfaces, March 26). Loni's synthesis included Pedro's point about avoiding scattered UIs verbatim.
- **Pattern**: When a VP echoes your point during their own synthesis, it means two things: (1) the idea was good enough to adopt, and (2) you were seen as the source. These moments compound over time. Document them. They are the evidence base for a promotion case.
- **How to create the conditions**: Speak early in a session with a clear, direct statement of position. Don't hedge. Don't add five qualifiers. Say the thing. The more specific and confident the statement, the more likely it is to be picked up.

## Operating Discipline

### Separate the Strategic Position from the Capacity Conversation
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Hero Surfaces one-pager. "Naming Experience Hub as the hero surface is a strategic position. What gets built on that foundation is a separate roadmap conversation."
- **Pattern**: One of the most common ways Director-level work gets blocked is by attaching strategic proposals to resource asks. Leadership can reject the resource ask and the strategy dies with it. Separate them. Get the strategic position validated first. Then have the capacity conversation.
- **Anti-pattern**: "We should be the hero surface, but we'd need two more engineers to do it right." The second sentence kills the first.

### Durable Themes Beat Brittle Roadmaps
- **Date identified**: 2026-03-26
- **Source**: Loni's direction in Session I and Session IV. AEM moving from "here are the 12 features we'll ship in H1" to "content management to context management" as the organizing frame.
- **Pattern**: In fast-moving areas (AI, platform evolution), 6-month feature roadmaps are liabilities. They create commitments you can't honor and expectations you'll miss. Directional themes hold. Features underneath them evolve. The theme stays true even as specifics change.
- **Application**: When asked for a roadmap, offer two things: (1) the durable theme that will be true regardless of what ships, and (2) the near-term bets that express the theme. This is harder to argue with than a feature list.
