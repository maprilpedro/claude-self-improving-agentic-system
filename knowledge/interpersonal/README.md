# Interpersonal Knowledge

> How to read, manage, and build individual relationships at work. This folder covers the one-on-one layer — the dynamics between two people — distinct from cross-org influence (systemic) or stakeholder management (strategic). The central question here is: how do you understand, work with, and influence a specific person?

---

## Reading People

### Confusing Warmth for Alignment
- **Source**: Live observation — Philippe Kapfer dynamic, April 2, 2026
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: A person can be warm, responsive, and collegial in direct conversation while simultaneously optimizing against your interests when an audience is present. Tone and behavior are different channels. Warmth in 1:1 is not evidence of alignment — it is just tone. The alignment signal is what they do when there's something to gain or lose in front of others.
- **The trap**: When someone is pleasant to work with, it's natural to extend them the benefit of the doubt. This creates a blind spot. You stop reading their moves because the relationship feels collaborative. The moment you stop reading is when they can act.
- **PM Application**: Separate the relationship register from the political register. You can enjoy working with someone and still track their behavior pattern objectively. The question to ask after any shared interaction: did they advance my position or complicate it? Do that over 3-4 interactions and the pattern becomes clear.

### How Political Types Signal Themselves
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 1 — Four Political Types)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre identifies four political orientations that determine how people behave in organizational environments. The Purist believes the work should speak for itself and distrusts any behavior that looks "political" — they get blindsided repeatedly and often become cynical. The Street Fighter plays to win at any cost, using information as a weapon and treating every situation as zero-sum. The Maneuverer is transactional: they'll help you today if it serves them tomorrow, and they track favors carefully. The Ethical Politician understands that organizations run on relationships and perception, and they operate politically without losing integrity. The type determines how someone will respond to proposals, who they'll support, how they'll behave when threatened, and what they need from you.
- **How to use**: Read the type before any high-stakes interaction. The Purist needs to see the work quality first — lead with substance. The Street Fighter needs to know you're not a threat or a target — neutralize or avoid. The Maneuverer wants a trade — make the exchange explicit. The Ethical Politician can be reasoned with directly. Wrong approach to wrong type is how smart people get blindsided.
- **PM application at Adobe**: Bertrand reads as an Ethical Politician — he plays the game but doesn't abandon principle. Most engineers are Purists who resent political behavior. Knowing the type changes how you frame the same message.

## Political Types & Personalities

### The Four Political Types in Detail
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 1)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Purist**: High integrity, low political awareness. Believes merit is the only currency. Becomes bitter when passed over for people who "played the game." Blind spot: doesn't realize that communicating your value IS part of the work. Will respect you more if you're direct and transparent.
- **Street Fighter**: High political awareness, low ethics constraint. Information is a weapon. They will take credit, deflect blame, and undercut you if it benefits them. Never show weakness or vulnerability. Don't share early-stage ideas with them. When they attack, respond calmly and with facts — they feed on emotional reactions.
- **Maneuverer**: Transactional. Favors are a ledger. They are not inherently hostile but will not help for free. Make the exchange visible. Don't expect loyalty beyond the current deal.
- **Ethical Politician**: The model. Politically aware AND integrity-driven. They build relationships, manage perception, and advance their agenda — but not at others' expense. They're the ones who get the most done in complex organizations because they work with the system rather than against it or above it.
- **Key test**: When threatened, the Street Fighter attacks, the Purist complains to the wrong people, the Maneuverer retreats to recalculate, and the Ethical Politician problem-solves. Watch how someone behaves under pressure and you know their type.

## Managing Up (Individual Relationship)

### Managing Your Boss — What They Actually Need from You
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 10 — Upward Influence)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Managing up is not about flattering your manager or making yourself indispensable through dependency. It's about understanding what your manager is accountable for and positioning yourself as the person who makes their accountability easier to carry. McIntyre's frame: your boss has a boss. Everything your manager does is filtered through what they need to demonstrate to the level above them. When you help them look competent and in control upward, you become valuable. When you create surprises, dependencies, or noise that complicates their upward relationship, you become a liability.
- **Practical rules**:
  1. No surprises upward. If something goes wrong, your manager hears it from you first, with a proposed path. Not from their boss, not from a peer.
  2. Understand their priorities, not just yours. What does Bertrand need to show Loni? That AEM's PM team is operating at a high level and that the AI investments are tracking. Work that demonstrates this lands differently than work that only demonstrates your personal excellence.
  3. Adapt to their communication style, not yours. If they like brevity, go brief. If they want context, give context. The burden of adaptation is on the person managing up.
  4. Make asks complete. When you need a decision, bring everything needed to make the decision: context, options, your recommendation, and the downside of each option. Don't make them ask you for more.
- **PM application**: Before every 1:1 with Bertrand, ask: what does he need from this meeting to feel confident in front of Loni? Start there. Your agenda is secondary.

### The View from the Top — How to Work with Executives
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 10 — Executives)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Executives operate differently. They are time-starved, pattern-matching, and always asking "so what?" They are not trying to be difficult when they cut you off or redirect — they are optimizing. The most common mistake when presenting to executives is leading with context, data, or process. They want: what's the situation, what are you asking for, and what happens if we don't act. Three sentences. Everything else is backup.
- **The credibility trap**: Executives decide very quickly whether you're someone they'll listen to or someone they tolerate. First impressions in exec settings are sticky. If you walk in nervous, over-prepared on irrelevant detail, or unable to hold your ground when questioned, you've set a baseline that is hard to recover from.
- **Executive currency**: They remember people who brought them a clean problem with a clear recommendation. They forget (or resent) people who brought them ambiguity, asked them to solve something without a proposed answer, or made them look uninformed.
- **PM application at Adobe**: Loni's time is scarce. She works in themes and directions, not feature lists. Every time you interact with her, the question is: did you make her smarter about something she cares about, or did you consume her bandwidth? Being the person who brings a clean signal is the entire game at that level.

## Managing Laterally (Peer Relationships)

### Lateral Influence — Getting Peers to Move Without Authority
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 10 — Lateral Influence)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Lateral influence is the most underrated skill in organizations. With peers, you have no hierarchy to invoke and no formal authority. Everything runs on reciprocity, relationship equity, and the perception that working with you creates value. McIntyre's principles: (1) Give before you ask. Build a balance in the relationship before you draw on it. (2) Make it easy to say yes. When you need something from a peer, frame the ask so that complying costs them as little as possible. (3) Find shared interests. The peer who seems resistant is usually protecting something specific. Find out what it is. If you can protect it and still get what you need, you've solved the problem. (4) Don't create debt. When a peer helps you, acknowledge it visibly and repay it quickly.
- **The alliance failure mode**: People who build alliances only when they need something have no alliances when they need them. The senior director builds relationships in advance of needing them, so when a critical moment arrives, the relationship already exists.
- **PM application**: Before cross-team alignment meetings (e.g., with Eugene's team, with Felix's team), ask: have I built any relationship equity here? If not, the meeting is starting with a deficit. Fix it before the meeting.

## Managing Down (Team Relationships)

### Downward Influence — The Secret of Effective Leadership
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 10 — Downward Influence)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre's counterintuitive point: the most effective downward influence is not direction — it's context. People who understand *why* something matters, what it connects to, and what the success criteria are, make better decisions at every level below you. The manager who over-directs creates dependency and compliance. The one who under-explains creates misalignment and rework. The right level is: explain the outcome you need and the constraints, then get out of the way.
- **The respect problem**: Technical teams (engineering, design, research) have low tolerance for PMs who exert influence through authority. They respond to demonstrated understanding and clear decisions. You gain influence downward by showing you know their world well enough to make decisions they can trust. You lose it by either demanding compliance without understanding or by being absent from decisions that require PM clarity.
- **What Senior Directors do differently**: They invest in being understood, not just in being obeyed. When a team understands what the PM is optimizing for and why, alignment happens without constant supervision.
- **PM application**: When briefing engineers on Experience Hub priorities, lead with the user problem and the success criterion, not the feature list. They'll design a better solution, and they'll own it rather than just executing it.

## Conflict and Adversaries

### Recognizing Supporters and Identifying Adversaries Early
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 4 — Recognizing Supporters / Identifying Adversaries)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Supporters and adversaries are not always the people who seem warmest or most critical in meetings. McIntyre's behavioral signals matter more than stated positions. Supporters: consistent follow-through on commitments, reference your work positively to others, flag threats before you encounter them, include you in conversations you should be in, and don't take credit for your contributions. Adversaries: agree in the meeting and undermine afterward, omit you from relevant conversations, frame your successes as team efforts and your failures as individual ones, raise concerns about your work to your manager without looping you in, and compete for credit in front of leadership.
- **The neutral zone**: Most people are neither supporters nor adversaries — they're watching. They will follow whoever seems to be winning. The goal is not to win over adversaries (rarely possible) but to grow your supporter count and keep the neutral majority from being recruited to work against you.
- **Timing**: Adversaries reveal themselves when you're succeeding. Supporters reveal themselves when you're under pressure. Early success signals and early setbacks are the best tests for sorting who's actually with you.
- **PM application**: In the Experience Hub context, apply this to cross-org stakeholders. Who consistently shows up for you in Loni's sessions vs who waits to see what Loni thinks before deciding?

### Dealing with Annoyances and Adversaries
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 4 — Dealing with Annoyances and Adversaries)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre distinguishes between annoyances (people who slow you down, misalign, or create friction without malicious intent) and true adversaries (people who will actively undermine you when it serves their interests). Most workplace conflicts are annoyances, not adversarial. Treating annoyances like adversaries escalates needlessly. Treating adversaries like annoyances leaves you exposed.
- **Handling annoyances**: Clarify expectations and norms explicitly. Most friction comes from people operating with different assumptions about process, ownership, or communication. A direct conversation early eliminates 80% of it.
- **Handling adversaries**: Don't confront them directly in public — that gives them a stage. Don't share information they could use against you. Do build relationships with the people whose opinions they value, so your credibility is established before they attack it. Do keep records of commitments and agreements.
- **The cardinal rule**: Never let an adversary see that they've affected you emotionally. Emotional reaction is the confirmation they were looking for. Respond with calm and facts. Return to the work.
- **PM application**: Before escalating any interpersonal tension to Bertrand, assess: is this an annoyance or an adversary? If annoyance, handle it directly first. Only escalate true adversarial behavior, and only with documented examples, not frustration.

### Converting Adversaries — When It's Worth Trying
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 4 — Converting Adversaries)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Most adversarial relationships exist because two people have conflicting goals or one person is threatening the other's path to success. Adversaries are not always enemies — they're people running a different calculation. Change the calculation and you can change the relationship. The conversion protocol: (1) Consider their point of view genuinely. What are their goals? Are you threatening them? (2) Test whether conversion is possible — people with common goals are potential allies unless one is a Sociopath or Dimwit. (3) If conversion is possible, have the direct conversation: agree to focus on the future, not the past, share each side's perspective without debate, identify strategies for assisting each other. (4) If conversion fails, increase your leverage so they cannot impede you. (5) If leverage-building also fails, direct containment — insist on clear boundaries of responsibility — and escalate only as an absolute last resort. Escalation involving higher-level managers always generates unintended consequences.
- **The adversary math**: Converting an adversary to neutral improves your situation twice — you lose an obstacle and remove a potential recruit against you. Even a modest chance of conversion is worth attempting.
- **PM application**: Before writing off any organizational adversary, run the conversion test: can I identify their goals? Are any compatible with mine? If yes, try the conversation. If no, move to leverage-building. Never skip straight to escalation.

### The Mirror Move — Being Made to Applaud Without Knowing It
- **Source**: Live observation — Philippe Kapfer side-chat during Loni meeting, April 2, 2026
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: A political operator doesn't always attack directly. The subtler move is to recruit you as a supporter without your awareness. The pattern: they open a warm side channel (text, chat, hallway), share their positioning news casually ("Loni just named my work as important"), wait for you to react warmly, then close with your validation baked in. You become a witness and endorser of their narrative — without ever agreeing to be one.
- **The moment it happened**: Philippe side-chatted Pedro during a Loni all-hands. He told Pedro that Governance/Enterprise Context was in his hands, Loni confirmed its importance. Pedro responded "very much on dirait hein cool!" — a genuine, reflexive reaction. Philippe closed with "C'est gentil mon loulou." Pedro had just endorsed Philippe's positioning to himself, in a recorded chat, without realizing it.
- **Why it's hard to catch**: It doesn't feel like manipulation. It feels like friendly chat. The warmth is real. The information-sharing is real. The effect — you as validator — is the political payload.
- **The counter**: Warm responses to positioning news should be brief and non-specific. "Cool" is fine. "Very much, that's great, you're so important" is validation you didn't intend to give. The rule: react to the person, not the positioning. "Sounds like a good meeting" vs "Loni really gets how important your work is."
- **PM Application**: Recognize when someone is sharing positioning news in a side channel during a high-stakes meeting. That combination — informal channel + strategic content + senior audience nearby — is always deliberate. Engage minimally and return to the main room.

### The Validation-Seeking Execution Bias — A Personal Pattern
- **Source**: Pedro Ferreira self-diagnosis, April 2, 2026
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Two linked biases that slow career progression: (1) measuring personal value by what you produce rather than what you influence, leading to constant need for external confirmation that the work was good; (2) defaulting to execution over framing — plonging into problems is comfortable because it leads to a deliverable and a validation signal. Framing is uncomfortable because it involves stating a position without a guaranteed result.
- **The effect**: You produce a lot, you help a lot, people appreciate you. And you stay at the same level. You are perceived as reliable and operational, not as someone who sees further than the work in front of them.
- **The two biases are connected**: The execution bias exists partly because it reliably produces the validation signal. Framing doesn't — you can cadre a problem well and still get pushback. So the brain defaults to the path with a known reward.
- **The AMISH application**: Awareness — notice when the impulse to solve arrives before the impulse to frame. Substitution — before starting to solve, write one sentence: "The real problem here is..." Motivation — every time you deliver without framing, you confirm the operational label. Every time you frame first, you shift it.
- **PM Application**: After any project debrief, ask: did I add a layer of insight to the brief, or did I just execute it well? The goal is to make the framing visible — not just the delivery.

### The AMISH Behavior Change Framework
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 6 — AMISH)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: When McIntyre's political suicide audit (Ch 6) identifies a behavior you need to change, she provides a five-step change model to make that change durable rather than temporary. The model: (A) Awareness — you must first be able to see the behavior happening as it happens, not just in retrospect. (M) Motivation — you need a reason that matters enough to sustain the effort. Abstract reasons fail; concrete consequences succeed. "If I keep doing this, I won't get promoted" is stronger than "it's unprofessional." (I) Identification — identify the specific trigger situations. The behavior doesn't happen randomly; it happens in particular contexts. Name them. (S) Substitution — identify the replacement behavior you'll use in those trigger situations. Not "stop doing X" but "when X arises, do Y instead." (H) Habits — practice the substitution until it becomes automatic. New behaviors feel effortful until they don't.
- **Why it matters for self-awareness**: Most behavior change fails because people identify what they should stop without designing what they should do instead. The substitution step is where most attempts fail.
- **PM application**: If the Anti-Seducer Audit (from Greene) or the political suicide warning patterns (McIntyre Ch 6) flag a behavior, run AMISH on it. Pick one behavior at a time. The awareness step alone is valuable — many people have never learned to see their own patterns in real time.

### Signs of a Toxic Workplace
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 5 — Signs of a Toxic Workplace)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre lists diagnostic signals that a workplace has crossed from "politically complex" (manageable) to "toxic" (survival territory): (1) Success is punished, not rewarded — high performers are undermined or pushed out because they threaten others. (2) Information is hoarded as power rather than shared as resource. (3) Credit flows upward and blame flows downward systematically. (4) There is no honest upward feedback — everyone tells leadership what they want to hear. (5) People leave not for better opportunities but to escape. (6) There is a visible inner circle that is impermeable regardless of performance.
- **Why it matters for PM**: In a toxic environment, standard PM behaviors (surfacing problems, proposing uncomfortable trade-offs, flagging risks, giving honest status) become liabilities. The organization is not optimizing for outcomes — it's optimizing for safety and status maintenance. Knowing which environment you're in changes what behaviors serve you.
- **The nuance**: Most organizations have pockets of toxicity within otherwise functional structures. A toxic manager inside a healthy org is still survivable. A toxic culture at the VP level is not — it flows downward.
- **PM application**: Periodically run this diagnostic against your current Adobe context. The indicators should inform how openly you share early-stage thinking versus how much you validate before socializing.

### How to Commit Political Suicide — Warning Patterns
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 6 — Political Suicide)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre catalogs the most common ways capable people destroy their own credibility and career momentum. These are not dramatic failures — they're quiet, accumulating, often invisible to the person doing them.
- **Pattern 1 — Burning Bridges**: Every person you dismiss, bypass, or humiliate is a future obstacle. Organizations are smaller than they appear. The person you ignored in the meeting today is the hiring manager for the role you want in two years.
- **Pattern 2 — Being Right in the Wrong Way**: Correcting people in public, winning arguments by humiliating the loser, demanding credit for being correct — these make you right and isolated simultaneously. There is a form of winning that destroys your ability to win anything important.
- **Pattern 3 — Overpromising and Underdelivering**: A single instance of this is recoverable. A pattern is fatal. The organization learns your commitments aren't bankable, and no one will stake their credibility on yours.
- **Pattern 4 — Misreading the Room on Timing**: Pushing a great idea at the wrong moment is indistinguishable from a bad idea. If the organization is in crisis mode, strategic proposals feel tone-deaf. If the leadership is just past a major decision, proposals that reopen it generate defensiveness. Timing matters as much as substance.
- **Pattern 5 — Failing to Maintain Upward Visibility**: Doing excellent work in a closed room is not the same as doing visible excellent work. If the people who promote you don't know what you're doing, you've opted out of the promotion conversation regardless of your output quality.

### Hazards of Uncontrolled Emotion
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 6 — Political Suicide)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre is direct about this: emotional displays in professional settings are almost always net negatives, regardless of whether the emotion is justified. This is not about suppressing emotion — it's about understanding what emotional displays signal to the people watching. Frustration signals loss of control. Excitement that becomes pressure signals that you've staked your ego on the outcome. Defensiveness signals insecurity about the idea's merit. The person who can receive criticism, setbacks, and conflict with visible equanimity signals strength and readiness for higher-stakes roles.
- **The asymmetry**: One emotional outburst in a senior meeting can undo months of credibility building. There is no equivalent positive emotional event that builds the same credibility in a single moment. The upside of emotional display is capped; the downside is not.
- **The specific Adobe risk**: In sessions where Loni's direction conflicts with your view, emotional pushback — even mild visible frustration — is noticed and remembered. The Senior Director response is to absorb the input calmly, ask a clarifying question, and return with a well-reasoned position later. Not to react in real time.

### Seeing Yourself as the Victim
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 6 — Political Suicide)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Victim framing is one of McIntyre's most quietly destructive patterns. It looks like: "I'm not getting the resources I need," "My ideas keep getting ignored," "Leadership doesn't understand what I'm doing," "I'm always the last to hear about decisions." These may be factually true. But framing them as things happening TO you removes your agency from the situation and signals to leadership that you are not operating at their level. Senior leaders are problem-solvers. They don't see the organization as a thing that happens to them — they see it as a system they navigate, influence, and shape. The person who consistently frames themselves as a victim will not be promoted into roles that require agency and influence.
- **The reframe**: For every "I'm not getting X," the Senior Director asks: what would it take for me to create X, or to make not having X irrelevant? The shift from passive to active framing is one of the most reliable markers of senior-level readiness.
- **PM application**: Audit your own language in 1:1s with Bertrand. If more than a third of your updates are about constraints, gaps, or things you lack — you are in victim framing territory regardless of whether those constraints are real. Balance it with what you're doing with what you have.

## Influence Skills (from McIntyre, *Organizational Politics*, Ch 9)

### The Paradox of Influence — Change Your Behavior First
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 9 — The Paradox of Influence)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre's central insight about influence: "To get other people to change their behavior, you have to change your own first." Most people approach influence backwards — they try to get the other person to change while keeping their own behavior constant. The paradox is that your behavior creates a significant portion of the other person's behavior. Change how you show up, and you change the dynamic without any direct request for change from them. This requires: (1) Self-observation — watching your own patterns neutrally, as if observing someone else. (2) Self-control — choosing your response rather than reacting automatically. (3) Selective behavior — deploying different behaviors in different contexts rather than having one default mode.
- **Application at Adobe**: Before any difficult stakeholder conversation, ask: what am I doing that might be producing the behavior I find frustrating? This is not self-blame — it's system-thinking. The conversation you've been having probably has a pattern. Break the pattern on your side, and the other person is forced to respond differently.

### The Influence Toolbox — Matching Method to Situation
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 9 — Influence Skills)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: McIntyre organizes influence approaches on a continuum from least to most assertive: Observe & Wait (gather information, watch patterns, hold your move) → Ask & Listen (understand their perspective before asserting yours; facts can be wrong, but opinions are simply different — never tell someone their opinion is wrong) → Persuade & Convince (present your case with evidence, find shared interests, make it easy to agree) → Order & Act (use positional authority, invoke process or policy, or simply decide and move). Most people default to either end of the continuum and miss the middle.
- **The calibration principle**: The appropriate position on the continuum depends on your leverage with this person and the urgency of the situation. With low leverage and low urgency, Observe & Wait is often right. With high leverage and high urgency, Order & Act may be necessary. The failure mode is applying high-assertiveness approaches when low-assertiveness would work better — it creates resistance and resentment unnecessarily.
- **The "facts vs opinions" distinction**: One of McIntyre's most practical points. In most workplace conflicts, the dispute is between different opinions, not facts. Treating someone's opinion as a factual error makes them feel dismissed and digs in the resistance. Saying "I see it differently" opens a conversation. Saying "you're wrong about that" closes it.
- **PM application**: Map your default influence approach. If you lead with persuasion in every situation, you're missing the power of asking and listening first. In cross-org alignment meetings, spend the first half asking questions and genuinely listening before presenting your position. You'll usually find that the resistance is rooted in something you didn't know, and your position can be adjusted to remove it.

## Emotional Intelligence in Professional Contexts

### Position Power vs Personal Power — Which One Actually Moves People
- **Source**: McIntyre, *Organizational Politics* (Ch 7 — Position Power vs Personal Power)
- **Date**: 2026-04-02
- **Insight**: Position power is the authority granted by your title and role — the ability to direct, approve, veto, or access. Personal power is the influence you carry regardless of title — people follow you because they trust your judgment, respect your track record, or want the relationship. In most organizations, position power is necessary to get things done on paper. Personal power is what actually gets things done when it matters. McIntyre's key point: position power without personal power creates compliance, not commitment. Personal power without position power creates a ceiling — you can influence but can't decide. Senior Directors have both, deliberately cultivated.
- **How personal power is built**: Track record of accurate calls. Consistent follow-through on commitments. Known willingness to be direct (including with uncomfortable truths). Reputation as someone who knows their domain without needing to prove it constantly. Relationships maintained over time, not just activated when needed.
- **The Director-to-Senior Director transition**: This transition usually requires a shift from position-power thinking to personal-power building. Directors often try to get things done through their role authority. Senior Directors build influence that works even before the title catches up.
- **PM application**: Where is your personal power currently strong? Where is it weak? Bertrand-relationship personal power is high. Loni-level personal power is being built. Cross-org peer personal power (Eugene, Felix, Shankari) is the active work. Title doesn't change this calculus — relationship equity does.
